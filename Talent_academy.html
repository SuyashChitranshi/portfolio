01

What I walked into.

When I joined this project, the problem had already been named — low course completion rates, employee complaints about content discoverability, and an L&D team struggling to manage and update content effectively. Three separate signals pointing at the same broken system.

The brief framed this primarily as a learner experience problem. My research quickly revealed it was also an admin problem — and that solving one without the other would leave the platform broken in a different way.

warning Core Pain Point

"I never know what I'm supposed to complete next. Courses are spread across different sections and there's no clear view of my progress or priorities."
— Anonymous Learner, Discovery Interview
Two user groups — two different problems
Employees / learners

Couldn't navigate what mattered

Content was undifferentiated. Learners had no way to tell mandatory courses from optional ones, no sense of where they were in a learning path, and no visibility into what completing a course actually meant for their role.

L&D administrators

Couldn't tell if anything was working

Admins were publishing content into a black box. There was no way to see which courses were being completed, which were being abandoned, or whether the curriculum was actually reaching the right people.

verified_user Constraints Faced

  • watch

    Time Constraints

    The platform had to be designed and rolled out alongside ongoing learning programs, limiting the ability to pause or restructure existing content flows. UX decisions needed to prioritize incremental clarity improvements over large-scale system changes.

  • architecture

    Technical Constraints

    The LMS was built on a legacy learning infrastructure with fixed role taxonomies, course metadata, and certification logic. This restricted deep personalization and required UX solutions that worked within existing data structures rather than redefining them.

  • psychology

    User Constraints

    Learners had highly varied experience levels—from fresh hires to senior engineers—making it necessary to balance simplicity for new users with depth for advanced learners, without fragmenting the experience.

  • business

    Organizational Constraints

    Design decisions needed to align with L&D governance models, approval workflows, and compliance requirements already in place. Stakeholder alignment across HR, engineering, and leadership influenced prioritization and scope throughout the project.

02

What I found — and what the brief had missed.

I conducted user interviews with 4–6 learners across different roles and seniority levels, and ran a collaborative discovery workshop with key stakeholders to surface assumptions and align on scope. Given the timeline, I made a deliberate choice to focus depth over breadth in the learner interviews — spending more time on a smaller group to understand the full task context rather than skimming across a larger sample.

Two findings shaped the entire design direction — neither of which had been fully surfaced in the original brief.

Finding 01 — Learners

Mandatory vs optional was completely invisible

Learners were spending time on optional courses while missing compliance-critical ones — not out of negligence, but because the platform gave no signal about relative importance. The content architecture treated all courses as equal.

Finding 02 — L&D admins

No feedback loop between content and outcomes

L&D admins had no visibility into completion rates, drop-off points, or whether role-mapped courses were reaching the right people. They were making curriculum decisions without any signal from the platform about what was or wasn't working.

Finding 03 — Both groups

Progress had no meaning in the existing system

Learners could complete courses and feel no sense of advancement. There was no structure connecting individual courses to role-level outcomes. Completion was an end in itself, not a step toward anything visible.

Finding 04 — System

The LMS infrastructure constrained deep personalisation

The platform was built on legacy infrastructure with fixed role taxonomies and course metadata. Any solution had to work within existing data structures — deep individual personalisation wasn't technically available.

03

The decisions I made — and one I couldn't.

Decision #1

Role-based learning paths that filtered content by job role

Rather than presenting every learner with the full course catalogue, I designed a role-mapped view that surfaced only the courses relevant to a learner's function. This directly addressed the discoverability problem — learners no longer needed to search for what was relevant to them. The role mapping used existing taxonomy data in the LMS, which meant it was buildable without backend changes. For L&D admins, it also created the first structured link between their content and specific role outcomes.

01. Maturity Models

Designers introduced a 5-stage learning maturity model (Aware, Equipped, Competent, Expert, and Certification) to provide a clear roadmap for skill mastery.

02. Progress Transparency

The use of 0% → 100% progress indicators, modular progress cards, and at-a-glance metrics (such as total learning time) helped reduce the cognitive effort required to track one’s own status.

Decision #2

A 5-stage learning maturity model to give progress meaning

I introduced a structured progression framework — Aware, Equipped, Competent, Expert, Certified — that connected individual course completions to visible role-level advancement. This solved the "progress has no meaning" finding by giving learners a destination, not just a completion percentage. It also gave L&D admins a framework to organise curriculum against — courses could be mapped to maturity stages, making it easier to identify gaps in the content offering.

Decision #3

A progress visibility layer for learners

I designed a persistent progress surface — modular progress cards, at-a-glance completion metrics, and clear mandatory vs optional labelling — that made a learner's status immediately readable without any navigation. This addressed both the "can't tell what's mandatory" finding and the broader sense that the platform gave no feedback on effort. In usability testing, task success rate for finding and understanding learning status reached 94%.

What didn't make it — technical constraint

Individual personalisation was vetoed by the data available

Early in the design process, I explored an adaptive recommendation layer that would surface courses based on individual learner behaviour and skill gaps — going beyond role-based filtering to true personalisation. L&D stakeholders were open to it, but the LMS infrastructure couldn't support it: the platform didn't have access to the individual skill or behaviour data the feature would have required. Rather than designing something the system couldn't deliver, I adapted the direction — leaning harder into the role-based path structure to create a form of structured personalisation within the constraints of what the data could actually support.

04

How I tested and what it told me.

Validation ran in two parallel tracks — structured usability testing with learners and regular design review sessions with L&D stakeholders. Both informed the design in different ways across the project.

🧪

Usability testing with learners

I ran moderated task-based sessions focused on the core learner journeys — finding a mandatory course, understanding current progress, and identifying the next step in a learning path. The 94% task success rate on finding and understanding learning status was a significant improvement over baseline observations of the old system. Sessions also surfaced edge cases in the maturity model labelling that I iterated on before handoff.

📋

Stakeholder reviews with L&D and HR

L&D stakeholders were involved at every major design milestone — not just for sign-off, but as a calibration check on whether the admin-facing features reflected how they actually managed curriculum. Several decisions about the admin dashboard layout changed directly because of these sessions, particularly around how course-to-role mapping was surfaced and edited.

05

Where it landed.

Launched

The platform launched following a 12-month design and development cycle. Post-launch signals confirmed the directional improvements — course completion rates increased, learner-reported mental effort decreased, and L&D admins reported meaningful improvement in their ability to understand and act on content performance.


The 94% task success rate in usability testing was the clearest validation signal during the project itself. The completion rate and perceived effort improvements are directional estimates validated with stakeholders rather than precise figures from production analytics — reflecting the scale of change rather than exact measured outcomes.


One gap the launch exposed: without individual personalisation, highly experienced learners still encountered role-path content that felt too basic for their level. This was a known tradeoff from the data constraint, but its frequency in the pilot was higher than anticipated — and is the clearest signal of where a future iteration should focus.

06

What I'd do differently.

Two things I'd change if I started this project again:

Involve L&D admins in research earlier

I came to the admin problem through the learner interviews — which meant I was already mid-design before I fully understood the admin side. Had I run parallel research tracks from the start, the admin dashboard would have been better scoped and less reactive to late-stage feedback.

Map governance constraints before designing

I underestimated how significantly L&D governance rules — approval workflows, compliance requirements, curriculum ownership — would constrain the design space. The personalisation veto wasn't a surprise in retrospect, but I lost time exploring a direction that a more thorough constraints audit would have ruled out earlier.

"This project taught me that enterprise learning platforms are two-sided products — and that designing only for the learner, without equal attention to the admin experience, produces a system that improves on the surface but remains broken underneath."
— Suyash Chitranshi
