Redesigning Fragmented Ecosystem

Our deliberate process for turning Complexity into Clarity.

01

I initiated this. Here's why.

This project didn't come through a product roadmap or a brief. A conversation with a senior manager about reporting delays made me start asking questions. The more I asked, the more I realised the problem wasn't one broken tool — it was an entire ecosystem of disconnected ones.

PMO members were managing delivery tracking across emails, spreadsheets, and manual documents simultaneously. Managers were waiting on evaluation submissions that had no status visibility. Timesheet approvals were happening outside the system entirely. Nobody had named this as a design problem. I did.

I put together a lightweight business case framing the operational cost of the fragmentation and got stakeholder buy-in to begin a structured discovery phase.

warning Core Pain Point

"I spend 4 hours a day just filtering through spreadsheets to track delivery progress because none of the reporting formats match."
— PMO member, initial discovery conversation
02

What I found — and what surprised me.

I conducted interviews and contextual observation sessions with more than 10 users across PMO, operations, and management roles. I also reviewed existing workflow analytics and ran a collaborative discovery workshop with key stakeholders to surface assumptions early.

Going in, the assumed problem was reporting inconsistency. What the research revealed was significantly broader: every major task in the workflow — evaluation submission, delivery tracking, timesheet approvals, and report generation — was taking far longer than anyone had estimated. The system wasn't just inconsistent. It was fundamentally unfit for the work it was supposed to support.

Finding 01

Evaluation submission was the biggest time sink

Users had to cross-reference three separate tools to complete a single submission. When we mapped the full task path, the number of steps was almost double what stakeholders assumed.

Finding 02

Managers had no visibility without chasing

There was no live status layer. Managers were sending follow-up messages to get updates that should have been surfaced automatically — producing an entire shadow communication workflow.

Finding 03

Timesheets were completed outside the system

Users had lost trust in the timesheet module and maintained personal records in spreadsheets, then back-filled the system — meaning system data was consistently stale.

Finding 04

Reporting formats varied by team

Without standardised output formats, PMO members manually reformatted data from different teams before they could act on it — adding 2–3 hours per reporting cycle.

The research also surfaced a tension I hadn't anticipated — one that would shape the core design challenge of the entire project.

Core design tension — two user groups, conflicting needs
Managers

Needed speed above all

Approval queues were long. Managers wanted one-click actions, minimal reading, and fast resolution. Any additional steps felt like friction.

Frontline users

Needed guided steps for accuracy

Errors in submissions had downstream consequences. Users needed confirmation states, clear field validation, and step-by-step structure to submit with confidence.

How I resolved it

Rather than designing two entirely separate flows, I used progressive disclosure and role-based views — managers saw a summary-first dashboard with inline approval actions, while submitting users went through a structured guided flow. Both paths existed within the same system, triggered by role context rather than separate interfaces.

03

The Decisions — and what didn't make it.

The core design concept was a combination of a role-based dashboard and contextual guided flows. Managers got a high-density summary view with inline actions. Users got a task-focused step-by-step interface that reduced cognitive load at the point of action. The system knew who you were and showed you the right surface.

Decision #1

Unifying four workflows into one ecosystem

The most consequential decision was consolidating evaluation submission, timesheet tracking, delivery monitoring, and report generation into a single platform — linked by a shared status model. Navigation was restructured around task type rather than module, which reduced orientation time significantly in testing. For the first time, PMOs had a single source of truth.

01. Cognitive Load Reduction

Resulted in Faster task completion and improved confidence in system usage.

02. Inline Actions

Allowed users to resolve issues directly from the dashboard without navigating to details.

Decision #2

Structured forms with mandatory field logic

Replacing freeform inputs with structured, validated forms directly addressed the inconsistency problem. Field logic meant certain fields only appeared when relevant, keeping the form shorter without losing completeness. Error rates in usability testing dropped substantially compared to the previous format.

Decision #3

Inline actions on the dashboard surface

Rather than requiring managers to navigate into a detail view to approve or flag items, I brought common actions directly onto the dashboard row. This was the single biggest speed improvement for the manager workflow — resolving the core tension between manager speed and user accuracy without removing required steps from the submission side.

What didn't make it — legacy constraint

A fully automated follow-up workflow wasn't buildable

Early on, I designed an automated notification and escalation system that would trigger follow-ups without any manual action — the cleanest solution to the shadow communication problem. But the legacy backend couldn't support event-driven triggers without a core re-architecture that was out of scope. I redesigned around the constraint: shifting from full automation to a semi-automated prompt system that reduced manual effort without requiring backend changes.

Stakeholder pushback — and how I responded

Managers pushed back on workflow simplification. I held my ground.

When I presented the simplified approval flow, two senior managers argued it removed controls they felt were necessary — their concern was that fewer steps would lead to uninformed approvals. Rather than compromising immediately, I ran a targeted usability test asking managers to complete approval tasks in both the old and new flows while I observed. The simplified flow produced faster completions with no increase in errors. I brought the test recordings and task-time data directly into the next stakeholder review. Both managers came around. The design shipped without the additional steps they had originally requested.

04

How I tested and what it told me.

Validation ran in two parallel tracks — structured usability testing with users and structured design review sessions with stakeholders. Running them in parallel rather than sequentially let me catch usability issues and alignment gaps in the same sprint cycle.

🧪

Usability testing

I ran moderated task-based sessions with users across both roles — PMO members completing a full reporting cycle and managers working through an approval queue. Sessions were observed and timed. Key signals were task completion rate, error frequency, and time-on-task. Findings were prioritised and actioned within the following sprint. Design revision cycles dropped from 11 to 4 per sprint as the design stabilised.

📋

Stakeholder reviews

I restructured the review cadence early in the project — replacing open-ended feedback sessions with structured walkthroughs tied to specific design decisions. Each review had a stated goal and a defined list of open questions I needed resolved. Approval rounds dropped from 5 to 2 by eliminating the ambiguity that had been driving repeated cycles.

05

Where it landed.

Soft-launched with pilot group

The platform soft-launched with a pilot user group following the 10-month design and development cycle. Early pilot feedback confirmed the directional improvements — dashboard adoption increased significantly, manual coordination effort reduced, and form error rates dropped. Full rollout is in progress.


The metrics shown are directional estimates validated with stakeholders, not exact figures from production analytics. Where precise numbers aren't available, the qualitative signal from the pilot is consistent: users are completing tasks with less friction, and PMOs are spending less time on coordination work the system is now handling.

06

What I'd do differently.

Three things I'd change if I started this project again:

Involve managers in research earlier

The pushback in the design phase would have been smaller if managers had been part of the discovery workshops. I learned their mental models too late, which cost time in the alignment phase.

Budget more time for edge case testing

Core flows tested well, but edge cases — exception states, partial submissions, cross-role handoffs — got less testing time than they deserved. Some surfaced in the pilot and required design revisions.

Build in an alignment buffer

Even with a structured review process, stakeholder alignment took longer than planned. I now treat alignment as a planned phase in any project I initiate — not a contingency.
